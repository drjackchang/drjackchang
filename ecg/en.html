Unit 1 · Core concepts

Cardiac Anatomy and Physiology

To work out from the ECG where the heart has a problem, you first need to know what the heart looks like, how blood flows and how the current travels. This unit is the map for everything we reason through later.

Introduction: what is an ECG?

The electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG) is a practical tool that lets us infer possible problems in each part of the heart by observing its electrical activity. EKG comes from the German Elektrokardiogramm. In 1903, Dutch physician Willem Einthoven invented the electrocardiogram, and he received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for it in 1924.

A sound patient assessment combined with efficient ECG interpretation leads to a more accurate differential diagnosis and appropriate care. Many clinicians have a love-hate relationship with ECGs: without understanding the principles behind them, they can't work out where a problem might be, so they fall back on memorizing, which is inefficient. This course focuses on the concepts you need for interpretation and offers a different way to learn ECGs.

Position and external structure of the heart

The heart sits directly behind the sternum, between the 2nd and 5th ribs.

From the outside, three major vessels sit on top of the heart. From the body's right to left they are the superior vena cava, the aorta and the pulmonary trunk.

The aorta is the only source of blood for the heart muscle: the coronary arteries, which branch off the aortic root, and their branches deliver blood to myocardial cells everywhere. When a coronary artery blockage leaves the heart muscle short of oxygen, the heart's electrical and mechanical function are directly affected.

The two prominent structures on either side of the upper heart are the left and right atria (singular: atrium). Below the atria are the ventricles.

Internal structure and valves

The heart has four chambers: the left and right atria and the left and right ventricles. The atria are separated by the interatrial septum and the ventricles by the interventricular septum, so blood does not flow directly between the two atria or between the two ventricles.

Valves separate the atria from the ventricles: the mitral valve lies between the left atrium and left ventricle, and the tricuspid valve between the right atrium and right ventricle. The semilunar valves lie between the ventricles and the aorta and pulmonary artery. Valves keep blood from flowing back into the previous chamber.

Myocardium and pericardium

From inside to outside:

  • Endocardium: the main lining of each chamber. The conduction system, from the SA node to the bundle branches, lies in the endocardial layer.
  • Myocardium: does the contracting and relaxing.
  • Epicardium: the outermost layer of the heart muscle, separating the heart from the other organs in the chest.
  • Pericardial cavity: the space between the epicardium and the pericardium, holding about 50 mL of lubricating fluid so the heart doesn't rub against the pericardium as it contracts and relaxes.
  • Pericardium.

The left ventricular myocardium is about three times as thick as the right.

Think about it: why is the left ventricle thicker?

The left ventricle has to pump blood into the aorta and out to the whole body (systemic circulation), overcoming high peripheral vascular resistance. The right ventricle only has to send blood to the lungs (pulmonary circulation), where resistance is much lower. The larger left ventricular muscle mass is also why the QRS is mainly determined by the left ventricle, and why the ventricular depolarization vector points down and to the left.

The heart's mechanical action is the contraction (systole) and relaxation (diastole) of the atria and ventricles, which directly drives blood circulation. Step through one cycle: watch when each valve opens and closes, and which part of the ECG it matches.

Systemic and pulmonary circulation

Systemic circulation: Oxygenated blood leaves the left ventricle into the aorta, flows through the arteries and capillaries of the body, then enters the venous system. The superior and inferior venae cavae return the deoxygenated blood to the right atrium.

Pulmonary circulation: Deoxygenated blood leaves the right ventricle into the pulmonary artery. After gas exchange in the alveoli, oxygenated blood returns through the pulmonary veins to the left atrium, ready to enter the left ventricle.

Note: the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood and the pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood. "Artery" and "vein" are named for leaving or returning to the heart, not for oxygen content.

The cardiac conduction system

The heart can contract on its own because it has a conduction system made of specialized cells (pacemaker cells). These cells don't do the mechanical work of contracting. Instead, they have two properties:

  • Automaticity: they can generate electrical impulses on their own, without outside stimulation.
  • Conductivity: special junctions between the cells let them conduct faster than ordinary myocardial cells.

Conduction sequence: SA node → internodal tracts (anterior, middle and posterior, plus Bachmann's bundle to the left atrium) → AV junction (AV node + bundle of His) → left and right bundle branches (the LBB divides into anterior and posterior fascicles) → Purkinje fibers.

A fibrous insulating layer separates the atria from the ventricles, and the AV node is the only way through. This keeps conduction orderly; any current outside these pathways can easily cause dysrhythmias.

Some people have an accessory conduction pathway, most commonly an accessory atrioventricular pathway: current bypasses the AV node and goes straight from the atria into the ventricles. This comes up with AVRT in Unit 7.

Autonomic nervous system: accelerator and brake

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) includes the sympathetic (SNS) and parasympathetic (PNS) nervous systems. They act in opposite directions and check each other, keeping the body in dynamic balance. In the cardiovascular system they mainly control cardiac output (heart rate and stroke volume) and blood pressure:

BP ＝ CO × PVR ＝ HR × SV × PVR

The SNS and PNS start from two control centers in the medulla oblongata. The cardioaccelerator (accelerator) connects through sympathetic nerves to the conduction system, atria and ventricles. The cardioinhibitor (brake) connects through the vagus nerve to the atria, SA node, AV junction and a small part of the ventricles.

Unit 2 · Cardiac Electrophysiology

Voltage changes in a single cell

What the ECG measures is the combined current from thousands of cells moving ions in and out at the same time. This unit focuses on a single heart cell: whichever channel opens, that's where the voltage goes.

Resting membrane potential (RMP)

When a cell isn't stimulated, ions still move across the cell membrane, but they stay in dynamic balance with no significant change in voltage.

Inside the cell there is a lot of potassium (K+) plus many large negatively charged molecules such as proteins. Outside the cell there is sodium (Na+), chloride (Cl−) and some calcium (Ca2+).Handout erratum

  • K+ diffuses out of the cell through leak channels, down its concentration gradient, leaving the negatively charged large molecules behind. That makes the inside of the cell negative.
  • Na+ slowly leaks into the cell. The sodium-potassium pump (Na+/K+ pump, which uses ATP) in the membrane moves 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in each cycle, maintaining the concentration gradients.

The voltage inside the cell then holds steady. This is the resting membrane potential (RMP). With negative charges lined up on the inside of the membrane and positive charges on the outside, the cell is said to be polarized.

Autonomic nervous system: accelerator and brake

The pacemaker cell action potential

Switch to "SA node pacemaker cell" above. Compared with a myocardial cell:

  • There is no distinct Phase 1 or 2; after Phase 0 depolarization, the cell moves quickly into Phase 3 repolarization.
  • Phase 4 is not a flat line but a slope that keeps climbing, and it starts from a less negative voltage (about −60 to −70 mV). So a pacemaker cell can rise to threshold potential on its own in less time. This is automaticity.
  • Pacemaker voltage changes are driven mainly by sodium and calcium moving in and out.

Once you understand the roles of ions and channels, you can work out which ion, which channel and which AP phase a cardiac drug acts on, and predict its effect and the patient's response. For example, β-blockers and calcium channel blockers flatten the Phase 4 slope and slow AV node conduction.

Refractory periods: ARP and RRP

The refractory period describes whether a cell can begin a new depolarization when a new electrical impulse arrives.

  • Absolute refractory period (ARP): from Phase 0 to the middle of Phase 3. During this time the cell will not depolarize again, no matter how strong the stimulus.
  • Relative refractory period (RRP): the second half of Phase 3. If a strong enough impulse arrives now, the cell may well begin a new depolarization.

Normally, the next impulse doesn't arrive until Phase 4. If an abnormal current happens to arrive during the RRP, it can disrupt the conduction system's normal order and cause a dysrhythmia. This concept comes up again in Unit 3 (R on T) and Unit 7 (the AVNRT re-entry circuit).

Unit 3 · How ECG waves form

Wherever the current goes, a wave appears

An ECG is a picture of the electrical current (depolarization and repolarization) that travels from the heart to the skin. Electrodes and lead wires carry it to the monitor, which processes it and draws the tracing. This unit answers one question: why do the waves look the way they do?

Einthoven's triangle and the leads

Einthoven defined a triangle made of three leads. Electrodes go on the right arm or right chest (RA), the left arm or left chest (LA), and the left leg or the left 5th intercostal space, midaxillary line (LL).

Each lead has one positive and one negative pole:

  • Lead I: RA (−) → LA (+)
  • Lead II: RA (−) → LL (+)
  • Lead III: LA (−) → LL (+)

Depolarization travels roughly from the SA node (near RA) toward the apex (near LL). That runs right along Lead II, so Lead II waves are mostly upright and the clearest. It is the lead used most for prehospital monitoring.

Detection rules and vectors

A lead detects the type, direction, strength and speed of polarization in the myocardial cells (which far outnumber the pacemaker cells). The rules are simple:

Toward the positive poleAway from the positive polePerpendicular to the lead
DepolarizationUpright waveDownward waveBiphasic wave
RepolarizationDownward waveUpright waveBiphasic wave

Countless cells in the heart conduct current in every direction, but the number of leads is fixed. So we use a vector: average the current direction of all the cells in one area to get a single arrow that shows direction and size.

Seeing the waves appear in order is not enough. P-QRS-T appearing in sequence only tells you every part of the conduction system is working. To judge whether the heart is working normally, you also have to look at how long each component lasts.

ECG paper

ECG paper is made of 1 mm × 1 mm small squares, and runs at 25 mm/sec:

  • Horizontal is time: 1 small square (1 mm) = 0.04 sec; 5 small squares make 1 large square (thick line) = 0.20 sec; 5 large squares = 1 sec.
  • Vertical is voltage: 1 small square (1 mm) = 0.1 mV; 10 small squares = 1 mV.

If a higher pacemaker fails, the one below takes over at its own rate (escape). So the rate itself is a clue to the origin.

Step 1: Calculating the heart rate

In the prehospital setting every second counts, so you don't count for a full minute. Instead you "estimate" the heart rate per minute (HR) from the ECG. Usually 6–10 seconds are printed (about 15–25 cm). Use the number of ventricular contractions (R waves or QRS complexes) as the heart rate, and avoid counting abnormal complexes (for example, ectopic beats).

It is best to calculate both the atrial rate (P waves) and the ventricular rate (QRS), so you can see how the atria and ventricles relate. The number on the monitor can't show the atrial and ventricular rates separately.

  • Six-second method (works for all rhythms): start at a thick line before a P wave, mark one second every 5 large squares, up to the 6th second. Count the P waves and R waves, and multiply each by 10.
  • RR interval method (regular rhythms only): 300 ÷ the number of large squares in the RR, or 1500 ÷ the number of small squares in the RR. You can use the P-to-P distance the same way to get the atrial rate.
  • 300 method (regular rhythms only): pick an R wave that falls on a thick line as the start. Each thick line after it is, in order, 300, 150, 100, 75, 60, 50, 43, 38, 33, 30. The number where the next R wave lands is the heart rate. If it falls between two thick lines, interpolate by the small squares.

Step 2: Checking regularity

Regularity mainly asks whether the R waves (QRS) come at a steady rate. The most accurate way is to measure each RR. The quick way is the paper method: line a sheet of blank paper up with any two R waves and mark them, then slide the marks along to compare with the other R waves. If any R wave is off from the marks by 2 mm (0.08 sec) or more, the rhythm is irregular.

The faster the heart, the smaller the error you can accept. Another rule says the rhythm counts as regular as long as the RR difference is no more than 10% of any RR length. You can use the same method on the P waves to check atrial regularity.

Irregular rhythms can be split into three types:

  • Occasionally irregular: happens now and then, for example premature beats.
  • Regularly irregular: repeats in a fixed pattern, for example AV block or bigeminy.
  • Irregularly irregular: no pattern at all, for example fibrillation.

Step 3: Looking at the P wave

The P wave represents atrial depolarization. With normal conduction, the P wave is a smooth, upright wave lasting about 0.10 sec, followed after a short delay by a QRS. When you analyze the P wave, look at its shape, duration, and relationship to the QRS.

A different origin of atrial depolarization gives the P wave a different direction and position. Tap the table to see:

P wave originLead II shapeP wave position

A notched P wave may mean left atrial enlargement: the left and right atria don't depolarize together (the left side is delayed). Sawtooth or completely irregular P waves are covered in the arrhythmia units.

Normally the atria and ventricles contract at the same rate, but some abnormalities make them differ. So when you look at the P wave, also calculate the atrial (P) and ventricular (QRS) rates separately, and check the P:QRS ratio: is there a QRS after every P? Is there a P before every QRS?

Step 4: Looking at the PR interval

The PR interval runs from the start of the P wave to the start of the QRS. Normal is 0.12–0.20 sec (3–5 small squares).

  • Less than 0.12 sec: atrial depolarization starts in the lower atrium or in the AV junction, very close to the AV junction, so the usual AV node delay is very short or absent. Or the impulse may be going straight into the ventricles through an accessory pathway.
  • More than 0.20 sec: most likely an AV block.

Besides the length, check whether each PR is constant. For example, in some AV blocks the PR gets gradually longer.

Step 5: Looking at the QRS complex

The QRS is ventricular depolarization. The QRS looks a little different in each lead, but its normal width is less than 0.12 sec (3 small squares). When the QRS is wider than 0.12 sec or has an abnormal shape, you can infer that depolarization did not take the normal path: it may start in the ventricles, or start above the ventricles with another conduction problem on top.

Step 6: Analysis

Use the first five steps to answer: Is the rate normal? Is it regular? Does PQRST appear in order (did the current follow the normal conduction system)? Where is the origin of depolarization? Is there a block in the conduction system?

Unit 5 · Sinus Rhythms

Sinus Rhythms

The depolarization current starts in the SA node and travels through the conduction system in order, so the P wave, PR interval and QRS are normal. Sinus rhythms are divided into five types by rate and regularity. Press "New strip" on any rhythm strip to generate a new one.

Normal Sinus Rhythm (NSR)

In a normal heart, the SA node sends out the depolarization current, which passes through the conduction system and produces all the normal waveforms. The heart rate is 60–100/min. NSR is always the first rhythm to learn: once you know what normal looks like, you can work out abnormal rhythms and where the problem is.

Normally, the pulse you feel has the same rate as the ECG. When the ECG shows NSR or another rhythm (except VF, VT and Asystole) but you cannot feel a pulse, this is called pulseless electrical activity (PEA): the mechanical system has stopped, but the conduction system is still conducting current. Treat it using the cardiac arrest protocol.

Sinus Arrhythmia

A sinus rhythm that is regularly irregular; all other criteria are normal. It is usually linked to the effect of breathing on the vagus nerve: on inspiration, vagal activity drops and the heart rate speeds up; on expiration, vagal activity rises and the heart rate slows. Handout erratum

The handout says "吸氣時胸腔內壓力增加壓迫到迷走神經" (on inspiration, rising intrathoracic pressure compresses the vagus nerve). In fact, intrathoracic pressure falls on inspiration; the main reason the heart rate speeds up is that vagal tone is suppressed during inspiration.

Arrhythmia is the older name for dysrhythmia. Both terms are still in use today.

Sinus Bradycardia

The only difference from NSR is a heart rate below 60/min. In general, bradycardia means any rhythm with HR < 60, and tachycardia means HR > 100.

Possible causes: overstimulation of the carotid sinus or a Valsalva maneuver (vagal stimulation), beta blockers (block the heart's beta receptors), calcium channel blockers (block calcium channels; recall the role of calcium in Unit 2), digoxin toxicity, acute myocardial infarction, and others.

Clinical presentation: the patient may have no symptoms (it is normal in some athletes), or may feel dizzy or faint. In severe cases, reduced cardiac output can lead to shock or cardiac arrest (BP ＝ HR × SV × PVR).

Sinus Tachycardia

A sinus rhythm with a heart rate above 100/min. Handout erratum: "竇性頻賣" should read "竇性頻脈" When the rate is very fast, the P wave may overlap the previous T wave.

Possible causes: smoking, alcohol, coffee, exercise, stress, pain, drugs that speed up the heart, heart disease, early shock, and others.

Clinical presentation: a faster heart rate uses a lot of oxygen and energy and increases the workload on the heart. This may raise the risk of myocardial infarction, or cause chest pain and shortness of breath.

Sinus Arrest and SA Exit Block

In both, the underlying rhythm is a sinus rhythm, with a sudden pause in the middle. The difference is the length of the pause. Turn on "Expected sinus timing" and compare against the dashed lines:

  • Sinus arrest: the SA node temporarily does not fire. A pause follows the T wave, and you cannot predict when the next P wave will come: the pause is not a multiple of the PP (or RR) interval, and the rhythm afterward "resets."
  • SA exit block: the SA node still has automaticity, but the current cannot get out. It cannot depolarize the atria, let alone reach the AV node. The pause is exactly a whole multiple of the PP interval, and the beats afterward still fall on the original beat.

Unit 6 · Atrial Arrhythmias

Atrial Arrhythmias

The depolarization current starts in the atria (outside the SA node) and does not follow the normal path or rate within the atria. But once the current enters the AV node, its path to the ventricles is still normal. So the P wave is abnormal, while the QRS and T are still normal.

Premature Atrial Complex (PAC)

A PAC is a P-QRS-T complex that comes early, outside the underlying rhythm. An atrial cell outside the SA node uses its automaticity to fire first. The current still passes through the AV node and the normal path after it.

The shape of P′ (the abnormal P wave) depends on where it starts: if the focus is near the SA node, P′ looks a lot like P; if it is near the AV node or in the left atrium, current travels both toward the SA node and toward the AV node, so P′ may be inverted or misshapen. The QRS-T is the same as in the underlying rhythm.

A PAC is usually followed by a short pause:

  • Non-compensatory pause: the normal complex after the early complex does not appear at the expected time. The PAC's current resets the SA node, and the rhythm starts counting again. PACs usually have this type.
  • Compensatory pause: the normal complex after the early complex still appears at the expected time; the SA node is not interrupted (common with PVCs).

Turn on "Expected sinus timing": the beats after the PAC are all shifted off the original dashed lines.

Atrial Flutter

The focus is in the atria, between the AV node and where blood returns from the superior and inferior venae cavae. The current forms a fast re-entry circuit in the atria, depolarizing the atria about 240–360 times per minute and drawing very distinctive sawtooth F waves.

Not every loop of current gets into the ventricles: the path below the AV node still works normally, and atrial current cannot enter if that path has not finished repolarizing or has not reached the relative refractory period. So the ventricular rate is about half the atrial rate or less (2:1, 3:1, 4:1).

Not every atrial depolarization produces an effective contraction, and the atria and ventricles do not contract in step. Less blood is pushed from the atria into the ventricles, and cardiac output may drop by up to about 25%. The patient may faint or develop hypotension or heart failure. Because the atria contract irregularly, blood may swirl along the uneven atrial wall and form clots, which can block blood vessels or cause a stroke.

Possible causes: heart muscle disease, hypoxia, heart failure, alcohol abuse, and certain conditions in middle-aged and older adults (for example, rheumatic heart disease with valve disease, or coronary artery disease).

Atrial Fibrillation (AF)

Handout erratum: the title "Arial" should read "Atrial" Depolarization currents start from many different sites in the atria (or form fast loops in the atria), each at a different rate. Atrial depolarization can reach 350–600/min. You cannot count it on the ECG; you only see fibrillatory f waves. Once the current enters the AV node, the path after it is normal, but the AV node can only let impulses through at random, so the RR interval is completely irregular.

The possible causes are similar to atrial flutter; it is most often linked to heart failure. It can also lower cardiac output or cause clots.

Unit 7 · Junctional Dysrhythmias

Junctional Dysrhythmias

The depolarization current starts in the area of the AV node and the Bundle of His. The current travels both upward (backward into the atria) and downward (to the ventricles), so P′ is inverted. Where it appears depends on which part of the AV junction the focus is in.

Where P′ falls reveals the focus

Premature Junctional Complex (PJC)

A complex outside the underlying rhythm, caused by current fired from the AV junction area (automaticity). The current travels backward toward the atria and SA node, and at the same time passes down through the AV junction along the normal path. So the QRS is normal, and the position of P′ depends on the focus.

An isolated PJC usually causes no obvious symptoms and can occur in healthy people. Possible causes: sympathetic stimulation or cardiac drugs, hypoxia, heart failure, coronary artery disease, and others. Very frequent PJCs may trigger more complex junctional dysrhythmias.

Junctional Escape Rhythm

The focus is in the AV junction, with a rate of 40–60/min. (Remember the intrinsic rates of the SA node, AV node and Purkinje fibers?)

When current from the SA node or atria does not reach the AV node in time, the pacemaker cells in the AV junction start firing to keep the heart working. The clinical picture is a lot like sinus bradycardia. This rhythm may also appear in patients after successful CPR, as pacemaker cells "wake up" one after another.

Junctional Tachycardia

The focus is in the AV junction, with a rate of 60–150/min: 60–100 is called accelerated junctional rhythm, and above 100 is called junctional tachycardia. Handout erratum: this figure and the next SVT figure are both labeled "圖 3"

Clinically, it is most closely linked to digoxin toxicity. It may also appear in patients after successful CPR because of the adrenaline given during resuscitation. Other causes: too much adrenergic medication, myocardial infarction that damages the AV junction, electrolyte imbalance (especially hypokalemia), and hypoxia.

Supraventricular Tachycardia (SVT)

SVT (including paroxysmal PSVT) can be further divided by where the loop is into AV nodal reentry tachycardia (AVNRT) and AV reentry tachycardia (AVRT). First, zoom in on the AV junction to see how normal current passes through.

Accessory pathways and AVRT

Once the loop forms, current from the SA node can no longer get in, and the loop takes control of conduction. The loop's path is much shorter than the normal path, so the ventricular rate is very fast, about 150–250/min. The current travels up to the atria and down to the ventricles at the same time, so the P wave is hidden by the QRS and cannot be seen. Handout erratum: the AVRT loop is not limited to the AV junction

Possible causes: excessive sympathetic stimulation, CNS stimulants such as amphetamines, alcohol, coffee, cigarettes, hyperventilation, or emotional stress.

Clinical presentation: the heart beats too fast for the ventricles to fill, which affects cardiac output and venous return (BP ＝ HR × SV × PVR).

Management: besides drugs, there is vagal stimulation (for example, carotid sinus massage) or the Valsalva maneuver: have the patient blow into a syringe plunger or similar to raise intrathoracic pressure. Baroreceptors on the aorta detect the rise in pressure, and the brain signals the heart to slow down.

NoteIf an accessory pathway activates the ventricles early during sinus rhythm, you may see a short PR and a delta wave at the start of the QRS (WPW pattern). This is one reason for "PR < 0.12 seconds" in step 4.

Unit 8 · Ventricular Dysrhythmias

Ventricular Dysrhythmias

The abnormal depolarization current arises in the ventricles, including the ventricular muscle and the conduction system within the ventricles. The current does not use the fast His–Purkinje pathway and can only spread from cell to cell, so the QRS is wide.

Premature Ventricular Complex (PVC)

An extra abnormal depolarization current fired from any area of the ventricles, outside the underlying rhythm. Because it arises outside the normal path, the QRS is wide. Because the current starts in the ventricles, there is only a QRS complex, with no P wave before it.

A PVC does not travel backward to reset the SA node, so it is often followed by a compensatory pause: turn on "Expected sinus timing," and the beats after the PVC still fall on the original dashed lines.

An isolated PVC usually causes no obvious symptoms and can occur in healthy people. Possible causes: sympathetic stimulation or cardiac drugs, hypoxia, heart failure, coronary artery disease, and others. Handout erratum: "PJC" in this paragraph should read "PVC" In patients with existing heart disease, PVCs mean that ventricular automaticity has increased or re-entry has developed, which may trigger more serious, life-threatening ventricular rhythms.

Ventricular Tachycardia (VT)

The focus is in the ventricles, with a rate of 100–250/min. No P waves can be seen, so you can think of the heartbeat as being driven by the ventricles.

Tip: the rounder wave is the T wave; the sharper one is the QRS complex.

It is usually linked to heart disease. The atria have little or no ability to contract, and with ventricular depolarization above 100/min, the circulation cannot supply enough blood. Without prompt treatment, the patient will gradually progress to cardiac arrest. The patient may still be conscious, but in most cases no pulse can be felt.

Torsades de Pointes

A special waveform of VT. The French name means "twisting of the points" (often described as a twisting ribbon). It is a polymorphic VT made up of several abnormal ventricular foci. The amplitude and direction of the QRS twist around the baseline in cycles. It is easy to mistake for VF, and you need to print a longer strip to interpret it.

It is often linked to existing heart disease, malnutrition or electrolyte imbalance, and long-term alcohol abuse. Since it is classified as VT, prehospital management is the same as for VT.

NoteA common trigger is QT prolongation (drugs, hypomagnesemia, hypokalemia). Hospitals often treat it with magnesium sulfate; in the prehospital setting, follow your service's protocol.

Ventricular Fibrillation (VF)

Like VT, a very dangerous and potentially fatal rhythm. Many depolarization foci in the ventricles fire chaotically at more than 300/min.

It may be linked to heart disease, drugs, hypoxia and other causes, and usually means the patient is already progressing to cardiac arrest. In VF, the whole heart almost completely loses its ability to contract. The circulation (including the coronary arteries) can no longer supply blood, while the ineffective contractions use up large amounts of the oxygen the heart muscle needs. How defibrillation works

The two defibrillation pads create a current path in the same direction as "SA node to apex." They deliver more energy than the heart's own depolarization, so the whole myocardium receives current of the same size and direction at the same time. This outside depolarization stops all spontaneous currents (normal or abnormal), and the SA node can then take control again.

Pad placement: one pad upper right (below the right clavicle, at the right sternal border), and one pad lower left (left 5th intercostal space, midaxillary line, at the apex). Handout erratum: the handout has left and right reversed

Accelerated Idioventricular Rhythm (AIVR)

A rhythm driven by the ventricular conduction system or ventricular muscle, with a rate of 40–100 (higher than the ventricles' intrinsic rate of 20–40). It usually appears in patients with myocardial infarction (especially after reperfusion), or in patients who have return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) after resuscitation from cardiac arrest.

The handout recommends treating AIVR with severe bradycardia or unstable vital signs as VT. Handout erratum AIVR is usually a temporary, protective rhythm. Antiarrhythmic drugs may suppress this only ventricular rhythm and leave the patient in Asystole. Manage it based on perfusion instead.

Asystole

From the SA node to the Purkinje fibers, nothing can fire a depolarization current on its own, and the heart has stopped completely. There are many causes; without prompt and proper treatment, they all end in Asystole.

In the prehospital setting, besides CPR, give resuscitation drugs such as Adrenaline and transport as quickly as possible. The heart has no electrical activity (even abnormal foci are gone), so defibrillation is not indicated.
